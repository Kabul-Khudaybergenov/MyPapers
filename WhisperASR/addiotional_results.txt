In‑text description for the manuscript:
“To further dissect the special‑letter errors, we constructed a confusion matrix that counts how often each special character was replaced by its plain Latin equivalent (Fig. 7). The pattern is clear: the model most often drops the acute accent (á→a, ó→o, ú→u) or the dotless i (ı→i), while substitutions among different special letters are virtually absent. This suggests that the decoder has learned the spelling rules but still lacks the confidence to maintain diacritics in acoustically ambiguous contexts. Targeted augmentation with words that differ only in diacritics could address the remaining 29 % of errors.”

Figure caption:
Fig. 8. Multilingual WER trade‑off for different fine‑tuning strategies. The pretrained model (PT) serves as a reference. Full fine‑tuning (FT) yields the lowest Karakalpak WER but increases error rates on Kazakh, Uzbek, and, to a lesser extent, Russian and English. Bottleneck adapter (BAFT) and low‑rank adaptation (LoRAFT) preserve much of the original multilingual accuracy while still substantially improving Karakalpak recognition, demonstrating a more favourable trade‑off for scenarios where code‑switching or multi‑language support is required.

In‑text description for the manuscript:
“Fig. 8 compares the multilingual recognition quality of the pretrained model with three fine‑tuned variants. The fully fine‑tuned model achieves the best Karakalpak WER (13.5 %) but suffers a clear performance regression on Kazakh and Uzbek, where the WER rises by 4.6 and 6.6 percentage points, respectively. In contrast, the parameter‑efficient methods BAFT and LoRAFT show only marginal degradation on these related Turkic languages while still reaching Karakalpak WERs below 19 %. This makes them attractive for applications that must handle mixed‑language input, such as public‑service kiosks or educational tools in Karakalpakstan, where both Karakalpak and Uzbek (or Russian) may be spoken. The results also imply that catastrophic forgetting mainly affects languages that share subword features with the fine‑tuning target, while distant languages (English) remain largely untouched.”
